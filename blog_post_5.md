# Critical self-assessment in this module

Over the past few months, I’ve been immersed in a course that focused on developing core academic skills—especially reading, understanding, and evaluating scientific papers. Now, the course is coming to an end and so are my blogs about the paper learning transferable visual models from natural language supervision. Time to evaluate my own performance, reflect on my learning process and plan the next steps.

## What did I learn in this course?

The course taught me not only how to read and understand complex papers, but also how to think critically about them. I’ve learned to identify the strengths and weaknesses of a paper and to form my own scientific opinions based on evidence.

## Can I read and understand a scientific paper now?

I’ve developed a better sense of what makes a scientific paper “good” and how to assess it. For example, I learned about the importance of metrics like the h-index, but also about the need to go beyond numbers and evaluate the content itself. Structured reading strategies have helped me to quickly grasp the key points of a paper and understand the broader topic it’s contributing to.

## Can I do literature research more effectively?

Definitely. Tools like scite.ai have been incredibly helpful in searching for relevant literature, but I’ve also realized that using these tools wisely is crucial. It’s not just about finding sources—it’s about evaluating their relevance and credibility. The course emphasized how important it is to double check the quality and significance of what you find.

## Can I put research into context with the current state of the art?

This is still a work in progress for me. As someone at the early stage of their research journey, it can be challenging to judge how a paper fits into the broader landscape of the field. Understanding what’s truly “state-of-the-art” requires ongoing effort—reading widely, staying updated on key topics, and making connections between different pieces of research.

## Can I form my own scientific judgement?

This is an important a difficult skill to develop. Forming a strong, independent scientific opinion requires deep subject knowledge. I’ve come to appreciate that before I can critically assess relevance or novelty, I first need to build a solid foundation in my chosen field. It’s a gradual process, but I now have the tools and mindset to start building that foundation.

## Can I present scientific results in different ways (Blog, orally, presentation, written document)?
Yes, and I’ve learned how important it is to adapt the format and style depending on the medium and audience. In written formats like blog posts or scientific papers, I now pay more attention to details such as meaningful titles, well explained images, and standard formatting like the IEEE style in LaTeX. When presenting orally, I’ve realized it’s crucial not to overwhelm the audience—especially with slides that are too packed with information. Instead, focusing on the core ideas, motivation, and methodology leads to a clearer and more engaging presentation. I’ve also learned to tailor the communication depending on whether the audience is made up of experts or non-experts. Throughout my Master’s studies, I feel I’ve already developed a strong foundation in scientific writing, and I consider that one of my strengths.

## Can I engage in a scientific discussion, express my view and formulate criticism?
I have learned for myself that the following points are important in order to engage in a scientific discussion, express my view or formulate criticism.

1.	Be clear and precise – Use clear, logical language and define key terms.
2.	Stay evidence-based – Support your views and critiques with data or credible sources.
3.	Be respectful and constructive – Criticize ideas, not people; aim to improve, not attack.
4.	Listen actively – Understand arguments of others fully before responding.
5.	Stay open-minded – Be willing to revise your opinion in light of new evidence or reasoning.


## How do I assess my performance, compared with my colleagues?
I believe I performed well and was able to keep up with the expectations. Everyone in the course put a lot of effort into their blog posts and discussions. What stood out was that most people didn’t just summarize papers—they really tried to explain the techniques and decisions behind them. Some of my colleagues expressed their thoughts more freely during discussions, which is an area I’m still working on. That’s why I would describe my performance as fulfilled, with room for improvement.

## What did I do better/worse than my colleagues?
Some of my colleagues wrote blog posts that were not only informative but also fun and engaging to read, thanks to their personal tone and humor. Their blog pages were also more creatively designed. Compared to them, I still see room for improvement when it comes to the layout and overall style of my blog. On the other hand, I noticed how much effort some classmates put into giving thoughtful and specific feedback. They didn’t just skim through posts. They really tried to connect the content to their own research.

## Where do I still have need for improvement?
One area I need to keep working on is building deeper expertise in my field. This is essential to contribute more confidently and insightfully to discussions. Participating in more conversations and exchanging perspectives with others will help sharpen my communication skills over time. Another challenge is forming a clear opinion about my own work. It’s often hard to step back and judge your writing critically, but it’s necessary if you want to produce high-quality, independent work.

## What could I learn from my peers?
I learned a lot just from seeing the range of papers others chose to analyze. Some of the papers weren’t the usual “big names” in the field, which made it even more interesting. It gave me the opportunity to discover new research that I probably wouldn’t have come across on my own. This reminded me how valuable it is to look beyond the obvious and explore a more diverse range of sources.

## Am I able to give & accept (constructive) criticism?
Yes, I’m very open to constructive criticism and always appreciate thoughtful feedback. It’s not always easy to give feedback to others, especially when it involves pointing out weaknesses. But I’ve come to see that honest, respectful critique is one of the best ways to grow—both as a researcher and as a communicator. Constructive feedback, whether given or received, pushes the work forward.

## What are the next steps?
I want to apply everything I’ve learned about writing and presenting scientific work to my next project (VT2). I also plan to share interesting papers and methods with others more regularly, as it helps to hear different perspectives and discover related research. Continuing this kind of exchange will be key to deepening my understanding and staying inspired in my field.

## Overall to the course
The module was challenging in the sense that you are not dealing with familiar tasks. For example, I had never thought about negative writing in a paper like this before. But when you have to face unfamiliar tasks, you learn things that you might not have noticed before.
